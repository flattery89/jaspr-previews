Are air purifiers safe to run all day? Ozone, noise and energy use explained

Before you leave an air cleaner running all day, check four things: whether it makes ozone, how loud it is at your speed, its power draw and its filter.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

A machine that runs around the clock owes you four straight answers.

You set it up, press the button and walk away. Then you think about it. It will run at midnight, through the workday and while you’re away for the weekend. Can you really just leave it on?

The honest answer depends on the machine. Four things decide it: whether the unit makes ozone, how loud it is at the speed you’ll actually run, what it costs in electricity, and whether you’ll keep up with the filter. None of it takes an engineering degree. Each one is a spec you can ask for or a sum you can do on the back of a utility bill.

We’ll go through all four, then where to put the unit so it works the way the spec sheet says it should. One note on scope. We’re not going to tell you how a machine will make you feel. We’re sticking to what can be measured: ozone, decibels, watts and filter dates. Where a figure comes from outside Jaspr, we say so.

Ozone: some machines make it on purpose

A fan-and-filter unit works by moving room air through a filter and sending it back. Nothing has to be generated for that to work. Ozone enters the picture because some devices sold for homes do generate it: an ozone generator creates it deliberately and pitches it as a way to freshen a room, and certain ionizers and other electronic technologies can give off some as a by-product .

The EPA advises against using ozone generators in occupied spaces , and California runs a certification program that limits ozone from air cleaners sold there . You don’t have to read either document. You just need to know what your unit does.

How to check a unit before you leave it running:

  • Read the listing for the words ozone, ionizer, "activated oxygen" or plasma. If any appear as a feature, ask what the machine emits and how that was measured.
  • Ask the maker directly. A clear "no ozone" is easy to give. A vague answer is also an answer.
  • Look for a third-party certification or test report you can read .

Jaspr is a fan-and-filter design with zero ozone . We list it as a spec, not a selling point. It’s the kind of thing a filter unit should state before you ask.

Noise: what 33 to 58 dBA sounds like when it runs all day

Noise is the reason people switch machines off. A unit that’s pleasant on a slow setting and loud on the speed it needs to be useful ends up unplugged. Ask for the dBA at the speed you’ll actually run, and the distance it was measured from. A top-speed figure tells you the loudest the unit gets, not what you’ll live with.

Decibels are logarithmic, so a gap that looks small on paper isn’t. A common rule of thumb is that every 10 dBA sounds about twice as loud . A quiet room is often put at around 30 to 40 dBA and normal conversation at around 60 . So 33 dBA and 58 dBA are not neighbors. One sits in the background, and the other is a fan you can hear from across the room.

What it costs to run: power and filters

A fan uses electricity, and a machine that runs all day shows up on a bill. The sum is short. Divide watts by 1,000 to get kilowatts, multiply by the hours it runs to get kilowatt-hours (kWh), then multiply by your rate per kWh, which is printed on your utility bill.

Monthly cost = (watts ÷ 1,000) × 24 hours × 30 days × your rate per kWh

For scale, a 100-watt light bulb left on all day uses 100 ÷ 1,000 × 24 = 2.4 kWh.

Watts change with fan speed, so ask for the figure at the speed you’ll run, not just the top one. Put those numbers into the formula and you have the answer for your home.

If the maker doesn’t publish watts, you can measure them. A plug-in power meter sits between the wall outlet and the unit and reports what it draws at each speed . Run it for a few minutes on each speed, write the numbers down, and you have the input the formula needs.

Questions to ask any maker about power:

  • What are the watts at each speed?
  • What are the watts on standby, with the display on and off?
  • Was the figure measured or calculated?

Electricity is half the bill. The other half is the filter. A machine that runs all day uses up its filter on a schedule, not on a whim, so ask how often it needs replacing, what that costs a year, and what happens to the warranty if you skip one. Cost per year = price per filter × replacements per year. It’s the number a sticker price leaves out. Add it to your electricity figure and you have what the machine really costs to run.

Where to put it

Where you put a unit decides how well it does its job. A few rules hold for any model:

  • Find the intake and keep it clear. Curtains, furniture and walls pressed against it cut down the air it can draw.
  • Put it in the room you use most. A room is the unit of measure: length × width × ceiling height, then air changes per hour = CFM × 60 ÷ room volume.
  • Close the door. A shut door defines the room you did the math for.
  • Keep it away from humidifiers. Owners report the sensor reading orange or red near one .
  • Think about distance from where you sit. A noise figure measured close to the unit can sound different from where you actually sit.

So, all day?

A well-made air cleaner is built to run continuously . What matters is whether yours passes four checks, and what a good answer looks like for each:

  • Ozone: a clear statement that it makes none, with a test or certification you can read.
  • Noise: dBA at the speed you’ll run, and the distance it was measured from.
  • Power: watts at each speed, so you can run the formula.
  • Filters: the schedule, the yearly cost and what happens to the warranty if you miss one.

Ask any maker for those four answers, including us. If a number is missing, that’s your answer.

Then test the unit in your own home for a week. Day one, listen at the speed you plan to use. Day three, note what makes it ramp up and how long it takes to settle. Day seven, check your plug-in meter and write the filter date on the calendar. A spec sheet tells you what a maker measured. A week at home tells you whether it works in your room.
