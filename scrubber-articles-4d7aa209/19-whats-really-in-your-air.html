Air quality

What's really in your home's air, and what you can do about it

Particles, gases and where they come from at home, and what source control, fresh air and a room air scrubber each do about them.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]5 min read

Your living room looks empty. The air in it isn’t.

Sunlight crosses the room and there’s nothing to see. No smoke, no haze. But indoor air carries tiny solid and liquid particles and a mix of gases. Some drifts in from outside. Much of the rest is made by ordinary life: dinner on the stove, a candle on the table, a spray under the sink, a blanket shaken out.

None of it is dramatic. It’s just unseen, and unseen things are hard to manage. This guide makes it visible: what’s in a typical home’s air, where it comes from, what each way of reducing it does, and where a room air scrubber fits.

We won’t tell you what any of it does to a body. We’ll tell you what it is, where it comes from and what moves it. We’ll also be plain about limits. A machine is not the first fix, and no filter removes everything.

Particles: what they are and where they come from

Particles are specks of solid or liquid small enough to hang in the air, and they’re sized in micrometers. PM2.5 means particles 2.5 micrometers across or smaller. PM10 reaches up to 10. For scale, an average human hair is about 70 micrometers wide, roughly 30 times wider than the largest fine particle.

Indoors, they come from heat and combustion: cooking, burning candles, fireplaces, unvented heaters and smoking. They also come from everyday dust (skin flakes, bedding and clothing fibers, pet dander), from pollen and other biological material, and from outdoors, where particles drift in through doors, windows and gaps. How much ends up in a room depends on outdoor levels, ventilation, indoor sources and what people are doing, which is why one home reads differently at breakfast and at midnight.

Damp rooms can add biological particles such as mold spores. If you have mold, the fix is to find and stop the moisture and have the mold properly remediated. Air purifiers are not a substitute for mold remediation.

Gases: the part that doesn’t show up as dust

Gases are a separate group. Volatile organic compounds, or VOCs, are chemicals that evaporate from certain solids and liquids. Common household sources include paints and varnishes, cleaning and disinfecting products, air fresheners, cosmetics, new furniture and building materials, dry-cleaned clothes, and printers and copiers. Some give off gas in storage as well as in use.

Combustion makes gases too. Gas stoves and unvented fuel-burning appliances are on EPA’s list of sources, and its advice is to vent them outdoors.

Gases matter here because they change what a machine can do. A filter built to catch particles doesn’t act on gases. Gases call for another material, usually activated carbon. Many air scrubbers pair the two in one cartridge. Jaspr’s does.

Three ways to reduce it, in the order we’d try them

Source control comes first. Take away or reduce the thing making it: a lid on the pan, unscented products, paint stored in the garage. EPA calls it usually the most effective approach. It often costs less than the other two.

Ventilation comes second. Bring in outdoor air with windows, or run exhaust fans that vent outdoors, especially while you cook or paint. One honest note: outdoor air has its own reading. AirNow publishes your local outdoor index, and on a day when it’s high, opening the window can bring the problem in rather than clear it.

Filtration comes third. A filter works on the air that reaches it, so two things count: how well it captures what it meets, and how much air it moves. EPA names both. Filters also need regular replacing. A loaded filter doesn’t do much.

Where an air scrubber fits

A room air scrubber is the third step, run alongside the first two. It’s built for what remains: fine particles already in the room’s air, odors, and whatever arrives each time a door opens. It won’t remove the candle or the sprayed air freshener. It can’t switch off a source.

Here is what Jaspr does. A grate circling the whole base draws air in from every direction. One cartridge holds a pre-filter, a particle filter and 1 lb of carbon. At fan speed 1 it moves 114 CFM (smoke CADR) at 33 dBA. At top speed it’s 340 CFM, up to 58 dBA. In a 2,000 cubic foot room that’s about 3.4 air changes an hour at speed 1 and about 10.2 at top speed. A yardstick, not a guarantee.

The built-in sensor raises the fan on its own when the air changes, and a Jaspr makes zero ozone. It also won’t remove everything. EPA says no air cleaner or filter will eliminate all pollutants in a home, and we agree.

Size it by volume, not square feet

Air doesn’t care about square feet. It cares about cubic volume. Multiply a room’s length, width and ceiling height to get cubic feet. Then take the airflow in CFM, multiply by 60, and divide by that volume. The result is air changes per hour.

The site suggests one Jaspr per bedroom, one in the living or cooking area, and one in a home office or gym. In a big or open space, start with the room where you spend the most time.

Read a number, not a mood

How would you know what’s in your room? Not by smell. Your nose adapts within minutes. Not by the look of the shelves either. The honest answer is an instrument. A standalone particle counter gives you a reading for the spot where you set it, at the moment you set it there.

When we quote a reading, we name the instrument, the room and the time. The screen on a Jaspr is a convenience, not the proof, and a reading is a clue, not a verdict.

The verdict: start with the source

Cut the source. Open a window when the day outside allows it. Then put a machine in the room where you spend the most time. That’s the order we’d follow in our own homes, and it’s the order the guidance gives.
