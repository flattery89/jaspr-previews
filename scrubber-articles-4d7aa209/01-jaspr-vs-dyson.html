Jaspr vs. Dyson: which premium air cleaner fits your home?

Jaspr and Dyson's Big+Quiet cost about the same. Here is how price, airflow, noise, filters and warranty compare, from each maker's own numbers.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]5 min read

Two machines near $1,000. Their spec sheets barely speak the same language.

You have two tabs open, one for Dyson and one for the Jaspr Air Scrubber. Both are tall, floor-standing and priced near $1,000. One page measures airflow its own way. The other uses CADR. One lists sound power. The other lists noise in dBA. A simple choice starts to need a calculator.

This comparison lines up price, airflow, noise, filters, warranty and proof from each maker’s own numbers. One maker is a household-name appliance brand. The other grew out of a restoration contractor’s job sites. Where Dyson comes out ahead, we say so.

We read the numbers in October 2026. Prices move, so check before you buy. Where the two companies measure things differently, we say that too. The Dyson here is the Purifier Big+Quiet Formaldehyde, the model closest to Jaspr in price. Dyson’s smaller Purifier Cool PC1 lists about 86 CFM at its top speed.

Price

Jaspr is $1,099 (list $1,199) with free shipping. When we checked, Dyson’s Big+Quiet Formaldehyde was listed at $899.99, down from $1,199.99, and showed as out of stock.

At the prices we saw, Dyson cost about $200 less. At list, the two sit a dollar apart. The sticker is half the story. Filters are the other half.

Airflow

Dyson publishes one airflow figure for this model: 87 liters a second, about 184 CFM, at the top of its 10 settings. It doesn’t publish the other nine. That figure is Dyson’s own "primary airflow" measure, not a CADR.

Jaspr publishes two, both smoke CADR: 114 CFM at fan speed 1 and 340 CFM at top speed. In a 2,000 cubic foot room (250 square feet, 8-foot ceiling), that is about 3.4 air changes an hour at speed 1 and 10.2 at the top. Dyson’s 184 CFM works out to about 5.5. The measures differ, so read these as a yardstick, not a scoreboard.

Dyson also rates the model for up to 100 square meters, about 1,075 square feet, from computer modeling at top speed with the unit in a corner. Jaspr publishes no room rating, so this article uses air changes.

The two also move air differently. Dyson projects its air outward at 50 degrees. Jaspr draws air in through a grate that circles the whole base, so it takes it in from every direction at once.

Noise

Jaspr lists 33 dBA at its lowest speed and up to 58 dBA at the top. Dyson’s spec sheet lists sound power of 39.8 dBA at setting 2 and 56 dBA at setting 10. It doesn’t list setting 1.

Those numbers don’t line up. Sound power is what a machine gives off. A meter in your room reads sound pressure, which changes with distance, and we couldn’t find the distance behind Jaspr’s figures.

Both are quiet at the bottom, and at the top 56 and 58 are close. A meter in one room, at the speed you would run, would settle it. Neither maker has published that.

Filters and running cost

Jaspr uses one cartridge: pre-filter, particle filter and 1 lb of carbon, swapped whole every six months in under 30 seconds. It’s $199 on the filter subscription or $299 one-time. That’s $398 or $598 a year.

Dyson uses three filters on three clocks. Its spec sheet lists a HEPA filter at 21,900 hours, carbon at 8,760 hours and a formaldehyde filter as lifetime. Run nonstop, that’s about 2.5 years for HEPA and one year for carbon. At the prices on a shopping listing, $169.99 and $109.99, Dyson’s filters come to about $89 a year at 12 hours a day and $178 running nonstop.

So Jaspr’s filters cost more than double Dyson’s, even with Dyson running around the clock. That is Jaspr’s weakest line. And if a named filtration grade is on your list, Dyson’s page gives you one. Jaspr publishes none.

Warranty and returns

Dyson’s warranty is two years, per its spec sheet. Jaspr’s is a lifetime warranty with free two-way shipping and a replacement unit, as long as you stay on the filter subscription and change the filter every six months.

That condition matters. The subscription keeps the warranty alive, which is part of why the filters cost what they do. Jaspr also offers a 30-day buy-back at the full price you paid.

Proof

Jaspr’s published lab report comes from LMS Technologies, a microbiology lab in Bloomington, Minnesota, released Aspergillus niger, a common mold, into a 4,000 cubic foot chamber at 71°F and 45% humidity. It sampled every five minutes and compared the unit against a run with it off. Corrected removal reached 97.1% at 60 minutes and 99.8% at 120 minutes. The lab recovered none from three hours on. The report doesn’t say which fan speed ran.

A lab chamber isn’t your living room; results depend on the room, the mold and how it’s measured. Air purifiers are not a substitute for mold remediation. Dyson’s room rating comes from its own modeling.

Jaspr also has more than 1,100 owner reviews on jaspr.co. Reviews are opinion, not a test.

Which one suits you

Dyson suits you if you want a named HEPA grade and a formaldehyde filter, a filter bill about half of Jaspr’s, and a lower price when it’s marked down. Its ten settings also give you finer control.

Jaspr suits you if the unit will run in a bedroom, where 114 CFM at 33 dBA matters. It also suits you if you want air drawn in from every direction through a grate around the base, a steel body, a one-part filter swap, a lifetime warranty, a lab report you can read and 30 days to send it back.

Jaspr’s built-in sensor raises the fan on its own when the air changes. Owners mention cooking and hairspray, and some report it reading orange or red near a humidifier.

Jaspr costs more to buy and more to run. If that gap decides it for you, Dyson is the better pick. If it doesn’t, try a Jaspr for 30 days and see.
