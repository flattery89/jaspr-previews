One room or every room? How to decide where your Jaspr goes first

One Jaspr cleans the air in one room. Here is how to choose where it goes first, what a second and third add, and what each one costs to run.

One Jaspr cleans one room. So which room gets it first?

You’re standing in the hallway, doing quiet math. The bedroom. The kitchen. The home office with the door that never quite closes. A filter cleans the air that moves through it, which makes a Jaspr a room-sized tool, and every doorway raises the same question: does this room need its own?

The honest answer isn’t "every room," and it isn’t "buy one and you’re done" either. It depends on where you spend your hours. This guide gives you a plain order for adding units, and says where one stretches and what each extra one adds to your running costs. Jaspr is one model in one size , so the order matters.

How one Jaspr works in one room

Jaspr draws air in through a grate that circles the whole base, from every direction at once, and sends it through one layered cartridge: a pre-filter, a particle filter and 1 lb of carbon. Whether that suits a room is arithmetic. Air changes an hour = airflow in CFM × 60 ÷ room volume in cubic feet.

At its lowest speed a Jaspr moves 114 CFM (smoke CADR). At its top speed it moves 340 CFM. In a 250 square foot room with an 8-foot ceiling, which holds 2,000 cubic feet, that is about 3.4 changes an hour at the lowest speed and about 10.2 at the top. A yardstick, not a guarantee.

Start with the room where you spend the most hours: the bedroom

The Jaspr site’s guidance is one per bedroom. The logic is hours. Most of us spend longer in the bedroom than anywhere else, and it is usually a closed room, which makes the math easy: a 12 by 14 foot bedroom with an 8-foot ceiling holds 1,344 cubic feet, so 114 CFM gives about 5.1 changes an hour.

It also has to be livable. At its lowest speed a Jaspr runs at 33 dBA, and the display has a dark mode, so there is no glowing panel by the bed. Noise at the speed you will run is the number to check before putting any unit in a bedroom .

Then the living or cooking area

The site’s second suggestion is one in the living or cooking area, and this is where the built-in sensor earns its place. It raises the fan on its own when the air changes. Owners report it ramping up for cooking, hairspray and toast smoke. One writes: "It’s now a game to see who can cook without making the living room Jaspr go crazy." (Angela O., owner review )

Open-plan spaces need a caveat. A living room that opens into a kitchen is one bigger volume. Two 250 square foot spaces joined make 4,000 cubic feet, where a Jaspr gives about 1.7 changes an hour at its lowest speed and about 5.1 at its top. Put it where you spend your time. One more owner note: some report the sensor reading orange or red near a humidifier, so place it with that in mind.

A home office or gym

The third suggestion is a home office or gym, where you spend long stretches behind a closed door. The same math applies, and so does the same honesty about size. A 10 by 12 foot office with an 8-foot ceiling holds 960 cubic feet, so 114 CFM gives about 7.1 changes an hour. A 400 square foot gym with a 9-foot ceiling is 3,600 cubic feet: about 1.9 at the lowest speed and 5.7 at the top.

So a small office is a natural fit, and a large garage or basement gym is a bigger ask. One owner writes: "I bought one for my office and one for my home." (Steve J., owner review )

Can one Jaspr cover two rooms?

Sometimes, with a compromise. A Jaspr weighs about 25 pounds, so it can move from room to room. One owner writes: "I use it mainly in my bedroom but have moved it all over the house during the day even to our finished basement." (Robin F., owner review )

That works if you accept that the other rooms get no filtering while it is elsewhere, and that a unit in an open doorway is working on two rooms’ worth of air. If two rooms are in constant use, the arithmetic favors one in each.

What each extra Jaspr adds

Be clear about the money before you build a plan. One Jaspr is $1,099 . Each one has its own cartridge: $199 every six months on the filter subscription, or $299 one-time. So each unit you add brings about $398 a year in filters. That is more than most rivals cost to run, roughly $50 to $350 a year for those we checked , and it is worth pricing in before you decide on three.

The lifetime warranty, with free two-way shipping and a replacement unit, applies while you are enrolled in the filter subscription and replacing the filter every six months . A 30-day buy-back at full price lets you try the first one before you commit to any others.

The order we would add them

Start with the bedroom you use most. Add the living or cooking area next, where the sensor does the most visible work. Then the home office or gym, if you spend long hours there. Stop when the rooms you spend hours in are covered, and don’t buy for rooms you pass through.

If one room is the priority, buy one and see how it fits your days. With the 30-day buy-back, the first purchase is the one that has to be right, and you get a month to find out.
