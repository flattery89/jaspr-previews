Everyday products that quietly add to your indoor air

Candles, air fresheners, cleaning sprays, new furniture, cooking and printers all add particles or gases to a room. Here's how, and simple swaps.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

Your candle, your plug-in and your new sofa have something in common.

Walk through your home with your nose and a list. A candle on the coffee table. A plug-in by the stairs. A spray under the sink. A new sofa in the living room, a printer in the study, dinner on the stove. None of it is unusual. Each one puts something into the air.

That’s the point of this guide. We’re not here to scare you off your own kitchen, and we won’t say what any of it does to a body, because that isn’t our place or our claim. Some everyday products release tiny particles, some release gases, and a few do both. Knowing which is the first step to choosing what to change.

EPA’s guidance on indoor air starts with the same idea. The most effective fix is usually to reduce or remove the source, then bring in fresh air, and last of all to filter what’s left. So we’ve organized this by source, with a swap for each.

One note on terms. "Particles" are specks of solid or liquid, like smoke and soot. "VOCs" are volatile organic compounds, gases that evaporate from certain solids and liquids. A particle counter sees the first group. A gas sensor sees some of the second. Neither tells you everything, and neither tells you what a number means for a person. That’s for the agencies that publish guidance.

Things that burn or heat

1. Candles and incense

Anything with a flame and a wick sends up smoke and soot, and those are particles. EPA includes burning candles in its list of indoor particle sources, along with fireplaces and unvented heaters. Incense does the same, and burns hotter and smokier than most candles. Scented versions add fragrance compounds on top.

Swap: Burn them less, for shorter stretches, with a window cracked. Trim the wick short. Keep them out of drafts, which make flames flicker and smoke. If you’re after atmosphere, a warm lamp or a simmer pot of water and citrus peel gives you the feeling without the flame.

2. Cooking

Cooking is a major everyday source of particles in many homes. Searing, frying and broiling send up the most. Gas stoves and other fuel-burning appliances also release combustion gases. If you’ve ever set off a smoke alarm with toast, you’ve seen what a hot surface can do in a small kitchen.

Swap: Turn on the range hood every time, and check that it vents outdoors. A recirculating hood returns the air to the room. EPA recommends exhaust fans that vent to the outside while you cook. Crack a window while you cook and for a while after. Use a lid when you can. If you’re searing, use the back burner, closer to the hood. And turn the heat down a notch when the pan starts to smoke. A smoking pan is a pan that’s too hot.

Things that evaporate

3. Aerosols and air fresheners

An air freshener isn’t cleaning anything. It’s air makeup. Sprays, plug-ins, gels and scented oils work by releasing fragrance into the room, and EPA lists air fresheners among household products that give off VOCs. Personal-care aerosols such as hairspray, dry shampoo and body sprays are in the same family: a product that works by getting into the air.

Your nose also stops registering a smell within minutes. Your guests’ noses don’t. So the fragrance you stopped noticing may be the first thing they notice.

Swap: Find the source of the smell. Trash, drains, pet bedding, damp towels, the diaper pail: the fix is usually to empty, wash or dry it. Then open a window. If you want a scent, choose one you can turn off, such as a simmer pot or a fresh-cut stem, rather than one that runs around the clock. For hair and body products, step into the bathroom with the fan on, or choose a pump or a cream over an aerosol.

4. Cleaning sprays

EPA lists cleaning and disinfecting products among common VOC sources. Spray bottles and aerosols also put a fine mist into the room. The more you spray, and the less the room is ventilated, the longer it lingers.

Swap: Spray the cloth, not the air. Use a pump bottle instead of an aerosol when you have a choice. For most everyday surfaces a damp microfiber cloth and plain soap does the work, and adds no fragrance. Buy smaller quantities, keep containers closed, and never mix products unless the label says it’s fine. EPA gives that last piece of advice itself. Open a window while you clean, and keep it open until the smell has gone.

Things that are new or running

5. New furniture, flooring and fresh paint

New things often release gases for a while after you bring them home. EPA lists building materials, furnishings and paints among VOC sources. Pressed-wood products such as particleboard and some cabinetry are a familiar example. So are the adhesives and finishes used to lay a floor.

Swap: If you can, unwrap and air out new pieces in a garage or on a porch for a few days before they come inside. Ventilate hard during and after painting or installing floors, and schedule the work for a time of year when you can leave windows open. Point a fan at an open window so it pushes air out, not around. When you’re shopping, look for third-party low-emission labels, and ask for solid or low-emission materials in rooms you’ll spend the most time in.

6. Printers and copiers

EPA lists office equipment such as copiers and printers among VOC sources. Some laser printers have also been measured releasing fine particles while they print. It’s a small contributor in most homes, and it’s easy to move.

Swap: Put the printer where you don’t sit, near a window or in a hallway rather than at your elbow. Print in batches rather than a page at a time. Open a window when you do.

How to see it for yourself

You don’t have to take any of this on faith. A standalone particle counter or a gas monitor will show you what each of these does in your own rooms. Set it on a shelf at about chest height and note the reading. Then light a candle, run the printer or spray the counter, one at a time, and watch the number. Open a window and watch it come back.

Treat what you see as a clue, not a verdict. A reading is one spot at one moment, and a monitor can’t tell you what the particles or gases are. But it’s the quickest way to find out which of your habits moves the needle in your home, and which doesn’t.

The five-minute swap list

  • Range hood on, every time, and make sure it vents outdoors.
  • Cleaning: cloth first, spray second, window open.
  • Skip the plug-in. Find the smell’s source instead.
  • Candles: fewer, shorter, with a window cracked.
  • New furniture: air it out before it goes in the bedroom.
  • Printer: move it away from where you sit.

Where a machine fits

None of this means you can’t light a candle or cook dinner. The point is to know what each thing adds, then pick the changes that cost you least. A room air scrubber is a supporting step after the source and the window, not a substitute for them. A Jaspr, for example, has a built-in sensor that ramps the fan up on its own when the air changes. Owners tell us they see it happen with cooking, hairspray and toast smoke.

Gases are the harder case. A filter built for particles doesn’t act on them, which is why a Jaspr cartridge includes 1 lb of carbon for odors. We don’t publish a gas-removal figure, so we won’t claim one. If a smell is the problem, finding and removing its source works better than any machine. EPA’s own guidance says the same: air cleaners help, but no air cleaner or filter removes every pollutant in a home.

Start with the source. Then the window. Then a machine for what’s left.
