Why dust keeps coming back, and what to do about it

You wipe it Saturday and it's back by Wednesday. Where dust comes from, why it resettles, what cleaning helps, and what a room air scrubber adds.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

You dusted on Saturday. By Wednesday, it looks like you didn’t.

The TV stand, the windowsill and the top of the fridge have all grown a new gray film, and the shelf looks like you skipped it. It’s tempting to conclude that you’re doing it wrong.

You’re not. Dust isn’t dirt you failed to remove. It’s a continuous supply. Every day, new material is made inside your home and carried in from outside, and whatever isn’t wiped, vacuumed or filtered out settles on the nearest flat surface. Cleaning handles what has already landed. It does nothing about what’s still on its way.

That changes the question. "How do I get rid of dust?" has a short, disappointing answer: you don’t. A better one is: where does it come from, what makes it resettle, and how do I cut down how much is in the air and on the surfaces? That’s what follows.

We’ll stick to what dust is and how it moves. We won’t make any claims about what it does to a body. The goal is a home that stays cleaner between cleanings.

Where household dust comes from

Dust is a mix, and the mix differs from one home to the next. The common ingredients are the same almost everywhere.

  • Skin flakes and hair. People shed constantly, and so do pets. Dander is the fine flaking from an animal’s skin and fur.
  • Fibers. Clothes, bedding, towels, rugs, upholstery and curtains all shed tiny fibers, mostly when you move or wash them.
  • Soil and grit from outside. It arrives on shoes, paws and clothing, and through open windows and gaps around doors. A large share of what builds up near entrances comes in this way.
  • Pollen and other outdoor particles. They drift in the same way.
  • Cooking and combustion residues. EPA lists cooking and burning candles among the sources of indoor particles.
  • Paper, packaging and craft debris. Cardboard and paper shed more than you’d expect.

Your home also makes dust each time you vacuum, shake out a blanket or walk across a carpet. Activity puts settled dust back into the air.

Then there’s the part nobody likes to say out loud: the tidier the house, the more obvious the dust looks. A dark table in a sunny room shows a day’s worth. A cluttered bookshelf hides a month. The amount in the air may be the same. Your eye is the instrument, and it isn’t a good one.

Why it keeps resettling

Particles don’t all fall at the same rate. Heavier, larger ones drop out of the air in minutes. Smaller ones can hang in a room for much longer. The bigger flakes are what you wipe off the shelf. The finer ones are still drifting.

Three things keep the cycle going.

  1. Movement. Walking, making the bed and opening doors stir settled dust back up, and the heating and cooling system moves air through the whole house.
  2. Still air. Dust lands where the air is calm: tops of cabinets, back edges of shelves, under the sofa and behind the TV.
  3. A dry cloth. Wiping with a dry cloth or a feather duster pushes some of it into the air, where it’ll resettle within hours.

This is also why a room can look cleaner and still feel dusty a day later. The surface got wiped. The air above it didn’t.

Dust, room by room

The mix shifts as you walk through the house, and so do the useful moves.

  • The entry. Mostly grit and soil, carried in on shoes. A mat and a shoe rack here save you more work than anything you do in the living room.
  • The bedroom. Mostly fibers from bedding, pillows, curtains and clothing, plus skin flakes. Shake out blankets outside when you can, and wash bedding on a regular schedule.
  • The kitchen. Dust here sticks, because cooking leaves a thin film of grease in the air that holds on to anything landing on it. Wipe the tops of cabinets and the fridge with a damp cloth and a little dish soap.
  • The living room. Upholstery, rugs and pet hair. A slow vacuum with attachments, and a damp cloth on the flat surfaces, covers most of it.
  • The home office. Paper and packaging shed fine debris. Printers add their own particles.

Cleaning methods that actually help

You can’t stop dust. You can make it easier to keep up with.

  • Clean from the top down. Dust shelves and fixtures first, floors last. Anything you knock down lands on a surface you haven’t cleaned yet.
  • Wipe damp, not dry. A slightly damp microfiber cloth holds dust where a dry cloth flicks it around.
  • Vacuum slowly, with a sealed machine. A vacuum with a sealed body and a fine-particle filter keeps what it picks up from blowing back out the exhaust. Go slowly over rugs. A quick pass leaves more behind.
  • Mop hard floors damp. A dry sweep is another way to put dust back into the air.
  • Stop it at the door. A mat outside and one inside, and shoes off, catch a lot of what would otherwise ride in.
  • Wash the soft stuff. Bedding, throws and curtains hold fibers. Wash on a schedule that suits your household.
  • Give it fewer places to land. Closed storage beats open shelves. Fewer ornaments means fewer ledges to dust.
  • Change the furnace or AC filter on schedule. EPA’s advice is to follow the manufacturer’s directions.
  • Brush pets outdoors, and wash their bedding regularly.

What a room air scrubber adds

Everything above works on dust that has landed or is about to. A room air scrubber works on dust that’s still in the air.

Here’s the idea. A filter only cleans the air that moves through it. A Jaspr draws air in through a grate that circles its base, so it pulls from every direction at once. It sends that air through one cartridge: a pre-filter, a particle filter and 1 lb of carbon for odors. At fan speed 1 it moves 114 CFM (smoke CADR) at 33 dBA, which changes the air in a 2,000 cubic foot room about 3.4 times an hour. At top speed it’s 340 CFM, up to 58 dBA, and about 10.2. Those are yardsticks, not guarantees. Furniture, doors and where the unit sits change how well a room mixes.

What it won’t do is just as important. It doesn’t stop dust from being made, and it doesn’t clean a shelf. Settled dust still needs a damp cloth. How much a Jaspr changes how often you dust depends on your home, so we won’t promise a number.

What you can look at, after six months, is the cartridge. It’s one home’s filter, so yours will look different. It shows that the unit moved air through it and caught particles. It doesn’t tell you which particles, and it says nothing about gases. A room with a lot of foot traffic, a shedding dog and a busy kitchen will load a cartridge faster than a quiet guest room, and that’s useful to know.

Placement matters too. A room air scrubber works well where the air can reach it, so don’t wedge it behind a sofa or in a closed-off corner. The site suggests one per bedroom, one in the living or cooking area, and one in a home office or gym. Start with the room where you spend the most time.

A realistic weekly routine

Nobody needs a dust schedule that runs their weekend. This one is short.

  1. Mats at the door, shoes off.
  2. Once a week: damp-wipe the flat surfaces, top to bottom, then vacuum slowly.
  3. As needed: wash bedding and throws, and brush the dog outdoors.
  4. On schedule: change the furnace filter and the cartridge in any machine you run.

It won’t make dust vanish. It’ll make Wednesday look more like Saturday.
