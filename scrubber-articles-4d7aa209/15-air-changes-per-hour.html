Why air changes per hour matter more than square feet

Square-foot ratings hide the ceiling and the air-change rate behind them. Here is the one-line math for checking any air cleaner against your own room.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

The square-foot number on the box hides two assumptions you never agreed to.

Up to 300 square feet. Up to 1,000 square feet. It looks like the one fact you need, and it tells you less than almost anything else on the box. Air doesn’t care about square feet. It cares about cubic volume, and the rating quietly assumes a ceiling height and an air-change rate each brand picks for itself.

There is a better number, and a tape measure and a calculator will get you there. It is called air changes per hour: how many times in an hour the room’s air passes through the filter. Here is the math, worked for a 250 square foot room at two ceiling heights, plus what it can’t tell you.

What a square-foot rating leaves out

A square-foot rating is a shortcut. The maker takes the unit’s airflow, picks a ceiling height, picks how many times an hour it wants the room’s air to change, and works backward to a floor area. Change either assumption and the rating changes, with the machine untouched.

Here is the same arithmetic with a hypothetical unit that moves 100 cubic feet of air a minute. Assume an 8-foot ceiling and one air change an hour, and it can be labelled "up to 750 square feet." Assume 4.8 changes an hour, the pace many room-size ratings are built around , and the same unit is labelled 156 square feet. One machine, two labels, both arithmetically correct, and a gap of nearly five to one.

That isn’t just a thought experiment. On October 5, 2026, one popular consumer unit’s product page claimed coverage of 1,882 square feet "in 1 hour" and, elsewhere on the same page, an AHAM-verified room size of 392 square feet . Divide the first by the second and you get 4.8. Neither number is false. They answer different questions, and the box doesn’t tell you which question it is answering.

The math in one line

Air changes per hour = airflow in cubic feet per minute (CFM) × 60 ÷ room volume in cubic feet.

Three steps:

  1. Find the room’s volume. Length × width × ceiling height. A 250 square foot room with an 8-foot ceiling is 2,000 cubic feet.
  2. Find the airflow at the speed you would actually run. Look for CFM. If the spec sheet gives cubic meters per hour, multiply by 0.5886; if it gives liters per second, multiply by 2.1189. A listing of 194 cubic meters per hour, for example, is 114 CFM. Use a CADR for smoke where one is published, and make sure a fan speed is attached. A figure with no speed is often the top one.
  3. Multiply the airflow by 60 and divide by the volume. That is the number of air changes an hour. Divide 60 by it to get the minutes for one full change.

A worked example: 250 square feet, two ceilings

Take a room of 250 square feet, about 20 by 12½ feet. Run it at an 8-foot ceiling and at a 10-foot ceiling, with a unit that moves 114 CFM at its lowest speed and 340 CFM at its highest. Those are Jaspr’s published smoke CADR figures.

Same floor, same machine. Raising the ceiling two feet cuts the air changes by a fifth: from 3.4 to 2.7 at the lowest speed, from 10.2 to 8.2 at the top. A square-foot rating can’t show you that, because it never asks about the ceiling.

For comparison, a hypothetical unit moving 200 CFM would give 6.0 changes an hour in the 8-foot room and 4.8 in the 10-foot one. If you are weighing two machines, put them through the same room and the same formula.

Airflow and filter quality work together

The number of passes is half the story. The other half is how much each pass removes. A simple model shows how the two interact. Take a well-mixed room with nothing new being added, and a filter that catches a fixed share of the particles reaching it on each pass. This is an illustration with made-up filters, not a figure for any product.

(The share left after t hours is e raised to the power of minus: air changes an hour × the share caught per pass × t .)

Read down the table. A strong filter at one change an hour loses to a modest filter at four changes an hour, 59% against 86%. The 97% row needs both. Airflow and filter quality multiply, and neither rescues the other. That is why a published airflow figure and a published test both belong on a product page, and why a very good filter behind a very slow fan is poor value. Real rooms aren’t well mixed and new particles keep arriving, so treat the table as a way to see the trade-off, not as a forecast.

What the number can and can’t tell you

Air changes per hour is a yardstick, and a yardstick has limits.

  • It isn’t a guarantee. The math assumes the room’s air mixes evenly. Furniture, closed doors and where the unit sits change that.
  • It counts passes, not results. It tells you how often air meets the filter, not how much each pass removes. A fast unit with a weak filter and a slow unit with a strong one can both disappoint. Airflow and filter quality both matter, so look for a published airflow figure and a published test.
  • It ignores what is being added. Cooking, a pet or a damp wall keeps adding while the unit works. The number tells you how fast the machine chases, not where the room ends up.
  • It depends on the speed. Quiet speeds give fewer changes. That is a real trade-off, so read the number at a speed you can live with.

Reading it honestly

Is 3.4 enough? It depends on the room and the job. Take the 4.8 changes an hour that many room-size ratings are built on as a benchmark. In this 250 square foot room, a Jaspr at its lowest speed sits below it and, at its top speed, well above it. The top speed also runs louder: up to 58 dBA, against 33 at the lowest .

Something else to know: at top speed, airflow doesn’t set one machine apart. Some cheaper units move as much air, or more. So a top-speed comparison alone won’t separate them, and the differences worth weighing are the others: airflow at the speed you’ll actually run, noise at that speed, what it costs to keep running, and whether anyone independent has tested it.

Check any room in five minutes

  1. Measure length, width and ceiling height, and multiply.
  2. Find the unit’s airflow in CFM at the speed you’d run, and convert if it is listed in other units.
  3. Multiply by 60 and divide by the room volume.
  4. Compare the noise at that same speed.
  5. Ask what the maker assumed behind any square-foot figure. If the page doesn’t say, do the math yourself.

Try it on a bedroom. A 12 by 14 foot room with an 8-foot ceiling is 1,344 cubic feet. A unit rated at 340 CFM, which is the same as about 577 cubic meters an hour, gives 340 × 60 ÷ 1,344, or about 15.2 changes an hour. The same unit at 114 CFM gives about 5.1, a full change roughly every 12 minutes.

Five minutes of arithmetic tells you more than a number on a box, and you can run it again for every room in the house.
