Particle filters vs. carbon filters: what each one catches

A particle filter is a net. Carbon is a surface things stick to. Here is what each layer catches, what neither can do, and why most machines use both.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

Burnt toast makes a haze and a smell. They’re two different problems.

You know the scene. Smoke curls out of the toaster, the kitchen goes hazy, and long after the haze clears the smell is still hanging around. That isn’t one problem. The haze is particles. The smell is gas and odor molecules. Each needs a different kind of layer.

Open an air purifier and you will usually find both, and both get called "the filter." A particle filter is a net. Carbon is a surface. A net does little for a smell, and a surface does little for dust. This guide takes the questions in order: which catches what, which handles odors, what neither can do and which one wears out first.

Which one catches dust, pollen and smoke particles?

The particle filter. It is a mesh of very fine fibers, and air has to pass through it. Particles get caught in one of a few ways: they hit a fiber head-on, they follow the airstream and brush a fiber as it bends, or, if they are small enough, they wander in a random path and stick to one. Different sizes get caught by different mechanisms, which is why filter makers talk about a most penetrating size, the particle size a filter finds hardest to catch.

The kinds of things this layer is there for:

  • Dust, lint and pet dander
  • Pollen
  • Soot and the fine particles that come off a pan, a candle or a toaster
  • Smoke particles
  • Mold spores, which are particles too. Air purifiers are not a substitute for mold remediation.

What does "HEPA" mean here?

It is a category term, short for high-efficiency particulate air. The common US definition is a filter that removes at least 99.97 percent of particles 0.3 microns across in a lab test . Europe uses a lettered system, such as H13, for a similar test .

Jaspr publishes no filtration grade. So this article can’t tell you where its particle filter sits, and it doesn’t imply that it meets any grade. If a published grade is what you are looking for, other machines publish one.

A grade is a result on a test bench. It doesn’t tell you how much air the machine moves, or what happens in your room.

Which one handles odors?

Carbon. Activated carbon is full of microscopic pores, so a small amount has a very large surface. Gas and odor molecules in the air stick to that surface and stay there. The word for this is adsorption: things stick to the outside, as opposed to soaking in like water in a sponge.

That is why carbon is the standard layer for cooking smells, pet smells and many fumes, and why it is the layer to look at if odors are your problem. Carbon doesn’t catch dust the way a net does.

Which one handles gases and fumes?

Carbon, with limits. Many volatile organic compounds, or VOCs, which come off paint, cleaning products and new furniture, are held by carbon. But carbon doesn’t hold every gas equally well. Very small, light molecules stick poorly to plain carbon, and some makers treat the carbon with another substance aimed at particular gases .

Carbon monoxide is one that plain carbon doesn’t hold well, so no air purifier is a stand-in for a carbon monoxide alarm .

Which one handles smoke?

Both. Smoke is a mix of particles and gases, and the two layers split the work. The particle filter handles the particles. The carbon handles the smell and some of the gases riding with them. Cooking is similar: fine particles and an odor at the same time.

Think of burnt toast. The haze in the air is particles, and the smell is gas and odor molecules. A particle filter works on the haze. Carbon works on what is left of the smell. Either one alone leaves half the job undone.

That is also why machines for kitchens and living areas tend to need both layers.

How do the two layers sit in a machine?

It varies by design, and the manual is the place to check. A common arrangement is a pre-filter first, to take hair and lint, then the particle layer, then carbon. Some machines put carbon in a separate panel you swap on its own. Others fold everything into one cartridge.

Why it matters to you: the arrangement sets how many parts you buy and how many dates you track. Three pieces on two schedules means three things to order and two reminders. One cartridge on one schedule means one of each. Neither is wrong. It depends on whether you would rather save money or save trouble.

Which one wears out first?

They wear out differently. A particle filter fills up. You can see it fill: the pleats go gray with lint and dust.

Carbon doesn’t change color. It works until its surface is occupied, and then it holds less. There is no visible sign, and your nose adapts to smells within minutes, so you can’t use it as a gauge. This is why makers put carbon on a time schedule. Its surface is finite, and the schedule is a way of replacing it before it is full rather than after.

The same logic applies to the particle layer, but the particle layer gives you a visible clue. Even so, a dark filter tells you air moved through it, not which particles it caught.

In the published schedules we looked at, carbon is often on the shorter clock. Jaspr combines both layers and a pre-filter in one cartridge, replaced whole every six months.

Two myths worth retiring

"A carbon filter cleans the air of everything." It doesn’t. It is a specialist: odors and many gases, with limits, and almost nothing for dust. A machine with carbon and no particle layer is missing half the job, and the reverse is also true.

"A grade tells you the machine will clean my room." A grade describes a filter on a test bench, for particles. It says nothing about odors, and nothing about how much air passes through the filter in an hour. Ask for the whole machine’s airflow too.

What neither layer can do

  • Neither fixes the source. A leak, a smoking stove or a damp wall keeps adding to the air.
  • Neither replaces ventilation, which brings in outdoor air.
  • Neither tells you what it caught. A used filter shows that air moved through it. It does not show which particles, and it says nothing about gases or how anyone feels.
  • Neither can be judged by the machine’s screen. The screen reads the air at a sensor. It is not a test of a filter.

How to shop for each

For the particle layer, look for a named test, not an adjective. For the carbon layer, look for a published weight. Jaspr’s cartridge holds 1 lb of carbon, per the product page , the page doesn’t publish a test for how it handles particular gases , and the machine’s smoke CADR is 114 CFM at speed 1 and 340 CFM at maximum. For any machine, ask for smoke CADR at a named speed, with units.

Pro tip: match the layer to the problem

Before you buy, write down what you are actually trying to solve. If it is dust and fine particles, the particle layer decides the result. If it is smells, the carbon amount and the replacement schedule decide it. If it is both, check both, and check what the maker admits it hasn’t tested.
