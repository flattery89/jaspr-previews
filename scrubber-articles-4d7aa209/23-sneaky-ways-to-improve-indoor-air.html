Sneaky ways to improve the air quality in your home

Fourteen small habits that change what ends up in your home's air: kitchen fans, shoes at the door, damp cloths, fewer sprays, and a few more.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

Your air changes every time you cook, tidy or take your shoes off.

Most advice about indoor air sounds like a project. Replace the carpet. Rework the ductwork. Buy something large and expensive. Some of it is worth doing. Most of it isn’t what makes a difference on a Tuesday evening.

What does, usually, is a handful of small habits. They’re unglamorous and nearly free. Each one cuts down a source of particles or gases, brings in fresh air, or stops something from being stirred back up. That’s the order EPA gives for improving indoor air: reduce the sources, ventilate, then filter.

Here are fourteen of them, grouped by where they happen. They’re sneaky because none of them looks like air care.

A word on what we’re not saying. We’ve left out anything about what air does to a body, because that isn’t our claim. These habits change what’s in your air, and you can measure that.

In the kitchen

1. Run the fan every time, and check where it goes

Cooking puts particles into the air, and gas stoves and other fuel-burning appliances add combustion gases. EPA recommends exhaust fans that vent to the outdoors while you cook. Many range hoods just recirculate. Look at where it vents. If a duct runs to the wall or the roof, it’s venting outside. If it’s a filter with no duct, the air comes back into the room.

2. Crack a window, and keep it cracked a while after

A window across from the stove gives the air somewhere to go. Leave it open while you cook and for a while after the heat’s off. It costs nothing, and it works better if there’s a second opening somewhere else in the house so the air can move through.

3. Cook with a lid, and use the back burner

A lid keeps more of what the pan gives off in the pan. The back burner sits closer to the hood, which gives the fan a better chance to catch what rises. Turn the heat down a notch when the oil starts to smoke. A smoking pan is a pan that’s too hot.

At the door and on the floor

4. Shoes off at the door, mats on both sides

A good deal of the grit in a home arrives on shoes. A mat outside, a mat inside and a spot for shoes stops a lot of it before it spreads. Pick mats you can shake out or wash.

5. Wipe damp, don’t dust dry

A dry cloth or a feather duster pushes dust into the air, where it settles again within hours. A slightly damp microfiber cloth holds on to what it picks up. Work from the top of the room to the floor, so you’re never cleaning something you’re about to cover again.

6. Vacuum slowly, with a sealed machine

A vacuum with a sealed body and a fine-particle filter keeps what it collects from blowing back out the back. Go slowly over rugs, and use the attachments on upholstery and along baseboards. A quick pass leaves more behind. Empty the canister or change the bag before it’s full, because a full one picks up less and can leak more fine dust back out.

7. Wash the soft stuff on a schedule

Bedding, throws, curtains and the dog’s blanket hold fibers and skin flakes, and they release them when you move. Washing them regularly takes that supply off the table. Shake out heavy blankets outside, not in the bedroom. If you can, give curtains a turn in the dryer on a no-heat setting to loosen what they’ve collected.

8. Brush pets outdoors

Brushing a dog or cat releases a cloud of loose hair and flakes. Do it on the porch or in the yard, and the cloud stays there. If you can’t take them outside, do it in a room with a hard floor and a window open, then wipe the floor damp afterward. A quick wipe of their paws on the way in helps with the grit.

With the products you use

9. Spray the cloth, not the room

EPA lists cleaning and disinfecting products among common sources of VOCs, which are gases that evaporate from certain solids and liquids. A pump bottle sprayed onto a cloth puts less mist into the room than an aerosol. Open a window while you work. And never mix products unless the label says it’s fine.

10. Skip the plug-in, find the smell

An air freshener isn’t cleaning anything. It’s air makeup. If a room smells off, look for the source: trash, drains, a damp towel, pet bedding. Fix that and the smell goes with it. Your nose stops registering a smell within minutes, so ask a guest what they notice when they walk in. They’ll tell you.

11. Air out new things before they come in

New furniture, rugs and fresh paint can release gases for a while after they arrive. EPA lists building materials and furnishings among VOC sources. If you can, unwrap them in a garage or on a porch for a few days first, and ventilate hard in the days after.

Around the house

12. Change the filters on time

A furnace or AC filter does its job while it’s clean. EPA’s advice is to follow the manufacturer’s directions on replacement. That goes for any machine in the room, too. A filter that’s loaded up doesn’t work well. Put the dates in your phone, and buy the next filter before you need it, so the swap happens on the day instead of next month. A Jaspr cartridge, for example, is a six-month swap that takes under 30 seconds with no vacuuming.

13. Let the air through

Don’t block supply and return vents with furniture, rugs or curtains. A vent with a sofa against it can’t move much air, and your heating and cooling system has to work around it. It’s an easy thing to check, and it takes thirty seconds. Walk the house once and look at every grille.

14. Look at a number

The sneakiest habit is checking. A standalone particle counter or monitor shows what the air in one spot looks like at that moment, and what changes when you cook, vacuum, light a candle or open a window. Treat it as a clue, not a verdict. A reading is one place at one time, and it can’t tell you what the particles are. Write the number down with the time and what you were doing. After a week you’ll have a pattern, and the pattern tells you which of these fourteen habits is worth keeping up in your house. [INTERNAL LINK: to the PM2.5, VOCs and AQI explainer, once published]

Where to start this week

Fourteen habits is a lot to take on at once, so don’t. Start with the three that cost nothing and change what you do every day.

  1. Fan on, every time you cook. Cooking is a daily source, and the fan is the control you have over it.
  2. Shoes off at the door. One habit, and the grit stops at the mat.
  3. Spray the cloth, not the room. Same clean counter, less mist in the air.

Add one more each week. By the end of a month it’s no longer a project. It’s how the house works. And if you own a monitor, you’ll see the difference in the numbers before you notice it anywhere else.

Where a machine fits

None of these fourteen needs a machine. A room air scrubber is for what’s left after you’ve done them: fine particles already in the air, or the moment dinner is on the stove and the window can’t be open. A Jaspr, for example, has a built-in sensor that ramps the fan up on its own when the air changes. Owners tell us they notice it with cooking, hairspray and toast smoke.

Start with the source. Then the window. Then a machine for what’s left.
