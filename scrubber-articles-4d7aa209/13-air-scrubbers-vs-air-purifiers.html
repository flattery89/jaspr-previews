Air scrubbers vs. air purifiers: how are they different?

Air scrubber and air purifier are not regulated terms. Here is where the names come from, what really differs, and the four numbers that settle it.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

Purifier or scrubber? The word on the box doesn’t promise anything.

You type "air cleaner" into a search bar and two words come back: air purifier and air scrubber. The towers look alike. The listings use both words as if everyone agreed what they mean. They don’t.

The two names have different roots, and the difference shows up in numbers you can check. This guide covers where each comes from, what separates the machines in practice, and when the cheaper, simpler option is the right call. Some of it is real engineering. Some of it is marketing, and we’ll say which is which.

A quick note on vocabulary. "Air purifier" is the everyday name for any room unit that filters air. "Air scrubber" is what restoration crews call their equipment. Neither term is regulated, so neither guarantees anything by itself.

What they have in common

Strip off the labels and the two machines do the same basic job. A fan pulls room air through one or more filters and sends it back. Nothing is added, and nothing leaves the room except what the filters catch.

  • Both recirculate. Neither brings in fresh outdoor air.
  • Both clean the air that moves through them. That makes airflow and filter quality count equally.
  • Both can be measured the same way. Clean air delivery rate (CADR) is a standard measure of how much filtered air a unit delivers per minute for a named pollutant: smoke, dust or pollen.
  • Both slow down when neglected. A loaded filter, a boxed-in unit or a room with the door open all reduce what either one does.

Where the two names come from

Purifier is a consumer appliance category: sold at retail, designed to sit in a living room, and priced and engineered around being compact, quiet and affordable. Those are fine goals. They also cap the size of the fan, the volume of the filter and the housing, which is why airflow is the number to check.

Scrubber is trade equipment: sold to restoration contractors who clean up after water, fire and mold, and designed around the job site. A contractor needs a lot of air moved, a deep filter stack and a machine that can run for days. A contractor doesn’t need it to be quiet, small or good-looking, and most aren’t.

The words have also drifted. Some brands now call a consumer unit a scrubber because the word sounds serious, and some purifiers move more air than products sold as scrubbers. The term describes a design intent, not a result, so treat the name as a hint and the airflow figure as the answer.

Five differences that show up in practice

  • Airflow. A scrubber is designed from airflow outward; a purifier is typically designed from price and noise inward. "Typically" is doing real work in that sentence, because plenty of purifiers move a lot of air. Compare CFM at the speed you would run, and treat any square-foot rating as a rough guide.
  • Filter stages and carbon. Scrubbers tend to separate debris, fine particles and odors into stages, with a real quantity of activated carbon for odors. Many purifiers use a thinner carbon layer, and many don’t publish how much. Ask for the carbon’s weight.
  • Build and duty cycle. Scrubbers use steel and heavy motors for continuous running. Many purifiers use lighter plastic housings and are fine for the job they were designed to do.
  • Noise and size. Professional units are loud and heavy. One widely used model is listed at 85 pounds with filters and rolls on a built-in dolly . They are sized for gutted basements, not bedrooms. A home scrubber has to solve that problem, and how well it does is a number: dBA at the speed you’ll run.
  • Cost to run. Filter prices vary more than anything else on the spec sheet. Several well-known purifiers cost roughly $50 to $350 a year in filters . A scrubber with a large filter can cost more.

Here is what an airflow gap looks like in a real room. Take 250 square feet with an 8-foot ceiling, which is 2,000 cubic feet. A compact unit moving 50 CFM (a hypothetical figure) changes the room’s air about 1.5 times an hour. A unit moving 114 CFM manages about 3.4, and one moving 340 CFM about 10.2. The formula is the same each time: CFM × 60 ÷ 2,000. A box could print the same room-size claim on all three if their makers assumed different air-change rates, and the arithmetic is what tells them apart.

What the label won’t tell you

Because neither term is regulated, the product page has to do the work. Look for four things.

  1. Airflow at the speed you’ll run, in CFM or CADR, with the pollutant named. A top-speed figure alone can flatter.
  2. Noise at that speed, in dBA, and at what distance.
  3. Cost to keep it running. Filter price, change interval and the yearly total.
  4. Independent testing. Who else has tested it, and under what conditions?

You’ll also see "HEPA" on many boxes. It is a filter classification, not a product type: a HEPA filter captures at least 99.97% of particles at 0.3 microns . It describes the filter material, not how much air the machine moves, so a HEPA label on a weak fan can still mean a slowly cleaned room. Jaspr publishes no filtration grade, and nothing in this article suggests its filter meets one. For a shopper who wants to compare grades side by side, that is a real gap.

Which one should you buy?

Be honest about the room and the budget.

A consumer purifier makes sense when:

  • the room is small and you won’t push it hard,
  • you want the lowest yearly filter cost,
  • you want a published filter grade to compare, or
  • you want to try an air cleaner before spending four figures.

An air scrubber makes sense when:

  • you want a unit built to run all day, every day,
  • odors are part of the job and you want a deep carbon stage,
  • you want a sensor that adjusts the fan so you don’t have to, and
  • you are comfortable with a higher price to buy and to run.

Think about where it will sit, too. In a bedroom, noise at the speed you would run matters as much as airflow. In a basement workshop or a garage, noise matters less and airflow matters more.

Whichever you pick, run the four checks on the product page first. The name on the box is a starting point. The numbers are the answer.
