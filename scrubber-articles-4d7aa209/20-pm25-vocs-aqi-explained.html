What your air quality reading is telling you: PM2.5, VOCs and AQI explained

PM2.5, VOCs and AQI look alike on a screen but measure different things. What each means, and why a reading is a clue, not a verdict.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]7 min read

Your monitor says 14. Fourteen of what?

Somewhere in your home there may be a number. It’s on a monitor, a phone app or the side of an air purifier. "PM2.5: 14." "VOC: moderate." A green dot that says "Good." It looks precise, so it’s easy to read as a verdict on your home. Then dinner happens, the number jumps, and you wonder what you’re actually looking at.

It’s a clue, and a useful one. Three terms turn up on almost every reading: PM2.5, VOCs and AQI. They sound like a set. They don’t measure the same thing.

One is a particle size, one is a family of gases, and one is an outdoor scoring system that indoor devices borrow. This guide takes each in turn, says what a reading can and can’t tell you, and ends with how to use one without over-reading it. We stick to what’s measured. We won’t tell you what a number means for your body, because a number on a wall can’t.

PM2.5: particles, by size

PM stands for particulate matter: solid and liquid specks small enough to float in air. The number is the size. PM2.5 means particles 2.5 micrometers across or smaller. PM10 reaches up to 10. For scale, EPA puts the width of an average human hair at about 70 micrometers, roughly 30 times wider than the largest fine particle.

Indoors, EPA lists cooking, burning candles, fireplaces, unvented heaters and smoking among the sources, plus biological material and particles that drift in from outside. So a PM2.5 reading often jumps when you sear a steak, light a candle or open the door on a dusty day.

Most home devices report PM2.5 as a concentration: micrograms of particles in a cubic meter of air. They usually estimate it with a light-scattering sensor, which shines a small light through a sample of air and measures how much the particles scatter. That’s quick and affordable, and it’s good at showing change. It’s an estimate, not a laboratory measurement. Some instruments count particles in size bins, and others convert a count into mass per cubic meter, so two devices can both be working properly and still disagree. Pick one and compare it with itself.

What a PM2.5 reading can tell you

  • How much fine particulate was in the air at that spot, at that moment.
  • Whether it rose when something happened: the toast, the candle, the vacuum.
  • Whether it settled afterward, and how quickly.

What it can’t tell you

  • What the particles are made of, or where they came from.
  • What the air is like in the corner across the room.
  • Whether any particular number is "fine" for any particular person. That’s a question for the guidance agencies publish, not for a sensor.

VOCs: a family of gases, usually shown as one number

VOC stands for volatile organic compound. EPA describes VOCs as gases emitted from certain solids or liquids. Its list of common household sources includes paints and varnishes, cleaning and disinfecting products, air fresheners, cosmetics, new furniture and building materials, dry-cleaned clothes, and printers and copiers. Some give off gas in storage, not just in use.

There are many different VOCs, and a home monitor usually can’t name them. It reports a combined figure, often labeled TVOC for "total", or turns it into a color or an index. Many consumer gas sensors respond to a range of compounds and can’t say which one moved the number.

A VOC reading is good at showing movement. Spray the counter and watch it climb. Open a window and watch it fall. It’s poor at telling you what the gas is, or whether a given level matters.

Reducing VOCs follows EPA’s usual order. Cut the source: buy less, keep containers sealed, use products outdoors when you can. Ventilate during and after. Particle filters don’t act on gases, so a machine that targets them needs a material made for the job, such as activated carbon.

AQI: an outdoor index

AQI is the Air Quality Index, EPA’s tool for communicating about outdoor air quality. The U.S. AQI runs from 0 to 500 and steps up through six color bands: green (0 to 50), yellow (51 to 100), orange (101 to 150), red (151 to 200), purple (201 to 300) and maroon (301 and above). It’s calculated for five outdoor pollutants: ground-level ozone, particle pollution (PM2.5 and PM10), carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide. EPA’s guidance for each band lives at airnow.gov, and that’s where it belongs.

Here is where it gets muddy. Many indoor devices show a color or a score styled after the AQI. Some convert their PM2.5 reading using the AQI’s scale. Others use a scale the maker invented. A green dot on a bedroom monitor and a green AQI in your weather app are not the same measurement, taken in the same place, from the same pollutants. Check the manual to see what your number is built from.

The AQI is useful in one practical way at home: it describes the day outside. Check it before you open the windows. On a day when the outdoor number is high, a cracked window can bring particles in rather than send them out, and the kitchen fan may be a better choice than the front door.

A reading is a clue, not a verdict

A reading is one spot at one moment. Move the monitor to the bedside table and it shows something else. Put it near the kitchen and it shows something else again. Steam, a humidifier or a spray can nudge an optical sensor without any change in the room’s particles. Owners tell us the sensor on their Jaspr can read orange or red near a humidifier. A reading moved. What moved it is a second question.

Here is how that looks in a real evening. The monitor sits on the shelf by the kitchen. Before dinner it reads low and steady. You sear something, and the PM2.5 line climbs fast. You turn on the range hood and crack a window, and it comes back down, though not to where it started right away. Nothing in that sequence is a verdict on your home. It’s a record of what dinner did, and what the hood and the window did about it.

So treat a number as a place to start looking. Take a baseline: read the same spot at the same time of day for a few days. Then change one thing at a time. Run the fan, skip the candle, crack the window. A reading becomes useful when you compare it with itself.

And remember what it can’t do. It can’t say what a level means for a person’s body, and we won’t try. That’s for the agencies that publish guidance.

Why we tell Jaspr owners to check the filter too

A Jaspr has a live air-quality display. It’s a convenience, and it isn’t the proof. The display can read "Excellent" on the day you plug the unit in, in a room it hasn’t yet had time to change, so a green screen alone proves very little.

The filter is harder to argue with. At each six-month change you lift the old cartridge out and see what the room’s air left in it. It shows the unit moved air through the filter and that the filter caught particles. It doesn’t show which particles, whether gases or odors were removed, or anything about how anyone feels. Pair it with a particle counter beside the unit and you have what the screen can’t give you: a reading from a named instrument, at a stated spot, before and after. EPA’s own advice is that a dirty, overloaded filter won’t work well, so swap it on schedule.

Using a reading well

  1. Name the instrument. Know what’s measuring, and what it’s measuring.
  2. Note the place and the time. A number without them is trivia.
  3. Compare it with itself. Baseline first, then change one thing.
  4. Follow it back to a cause. What was happening when it moved?

A number gives you a place to start looking. It doesn’t finish the job.
