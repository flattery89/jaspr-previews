8 common myths and misconceptions about air purifiers

Eight air purifier myths, from the screen that says Excellent to the bigger square-foot rating, with what is actually going on and the math to check it.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

A glowing screen and a big number feel like proof. They’re just easy to see.

You plug it in and the display glows green. The box said it covers a room bigger than yours. So you figure that’s settled and get on with your day. Nothing about that is foolish. It’s what the product puts in front of you, so it’s what you go on.

The mechanism is simple: a fan pulls air through a filter. Around it sits a thick layer of box claims and rules of thumb that don’t survive contact with a tape measure. Here are eight we hear often, what is really going on, and a note where the truth is less flattering than a brand might like.

A note on terms: "air purifier" here means any room unit that filters air.

1. "The screen says Excellent, so it is working."

A display reads one sensor in one spot. It can’t see everything in the air, and it can read fine on day one in a room that has a problem it doesn’t measure. Jaspr’s own display can say "Excellent" the moment it is switched on, and we would rather you knew that than learned it from a review.

The screen is a useful dashboard, not proof. For proof, use something separate: a handheld particle counter or a standalone air monitor, read before and after, in the same spot, with the unit left on one steady speed. Hold the conditions steady when you test: the same room, the same spot, the same speed and the door closed, and give it time before the second reading. Cooking or opening a window in between will swamp whatever the unit did. The filter you pull out at the change shows what it collected, though not how much of what was in the air.

2. "A bigger square-foot rating is better."

A square-foot rating is airflow in disguise. It is divided by a ceiling height and an air-change rate the brand chose, and brands choose differently. A unit rated for 500 square feet on an 8-foot assumption is facing 6,000 cubic feet, not 4,000, if your ceiling is 12 feet. That is half again as much air to work through, and its air changes an hour fall by a third. If a box doesn’t state the ceiling height and air-change rate behind its number, it can’t be compared with the next box.

Compare airflow in CFM at the speed you would run, then do your own math: CFM × 60 ÷ room volume. A 250 square foot room with an 8-foot ceiling is 2,000 cubic feet, so 114 CFM gives about 3.4 changes an hour and 340 CFM gives about 10.2. Air doesn’t care about square feet. It cares about cubic volume.

3. "Ozone means it is cleaning harder."

Some devices, notably ozone generators and certain ionizers, release ozone on purpose and market the fresh smell that follows. Ozone is a reactive gas. An air cleaner is supposed to take things out of the air, not put one in. Guidance from the EPA advises against using ozone generators sold as air cleaners in occupied spaces , and California limits ozone emissions from indoor air cleaners .

If a unit has an ionizer or a similar feature, ask whether it can emit ozone as a by-product and what the maker measured. A clear answer is a good sign. Silence isn’t. Check the spec sheet for a plain statement of ozone output. Jaspr produces zero ozone. A fresh smell after a unit has been running is not proof of cleaning.

4. "More filters stacked means cleaner air."

Stages help when each does a different job: a pre-filter to protect, a particle filter for fine particles, carbon for odors. But every layer is resistance, and the fan has to push air through all of them. Stack too many layers on a weak fan and the air slows down, and the room’s air gets through the filter less often. Each added layer catches something, and each one slows the flow. Where the balance lands depends on the fan, which a layer count doesn’t show.

A "7-stage" label tells you how many layers there are. It doesn’t tell you how much air moves. Ask for the CFM, and ask what each stage is for. If a stage has no job you can name, it is a sticker.

5. "A purifier replaces fixing the source."

A damp wall, a leak, a pet’s bed, a stove with a weak vent hood: these keep adding to the air while the unit works. A unit can keep up with a source. It can’t end one. Fixing the source and running an air cleaner aren’t rivals; they are a sequence. In a kitchen the fix is a vent hood that works, in a basement it is a repaired leak or a dehumidifier, and in a house with a pet it is a regular wash of the bedding.

Mold is the clearest case. It grows where there is moisture, and it is fixed at the source: stop the water, remove what is growing. Air purifiers are not a substitute for mold remediation. What an air scrubber can do is work on spores that have already reached the air, during and after the fix, and a good one gets to that air faster the more of it moves through the filter.

6. "One unit will handle the whole house."

A room unit works on the air in its room, and on the rooms its air can reach through open doors. Floors and walls matter as much as square footage: a unit upstairs does little for the kitchen below it. Run the numbers for a whole floor. A 2,000 square foot single floor with 8-foot ceilings is 16,000 cubic feet. A unit moving 114 CFM gives about 0.4 air changes an hour there, and one moving 340 CFM gives about 1.3.

That is why Jaspr’s own guidance is one per bedroom, one in the living or cooking area and one in a home office or gym, not one for the house. If a single unit has to cover several rooms, start with the room where you spend the most hours and treat the rest as a bonus.

7. "Every home has the same pollutants, so any unit will do."

A home with a gas range, two dogs and a damp basement has a different list from a new apartment. A new build with a dog needs odors and particles handled; an older house with a damp crawlspace needs the damp handled first. Dust, pet hair and cooking particles are the particle filter’s job. Odors and many gases are the carbon’s job, and the two are different jobs.

So check both. For particles, ask for the airflow and any published test. For odors, ask how much carbon the unit holds and how often it is replaced. A thin carbon layer can fill up and stop working long before the particle filter looks tired.

8. "Once it is plugged in, you are done."

A unit boxed in by furniture, switched off most of the day or running a loaded filter is doing a fraction of its rated work. Filters load, and a loaded filter lets less air through. Leave open floor around the unit, run it continuously at a speed you can’t hear, and put the filter change in your calendar the day you buy it. Set the routine once and the unit does its job without a second thought.

Maintenance is also a price question. Filter costs run from roughly $50 to $350 a year across the rivals we checked , and a filter you can’t afford is a filter you won’t change. Ask for the yearly number before you buy.

What to take from it

Most of these myths share a root: a number or a label standing in for a measurement. The cure is the same each time. Ask for airflow at the speed you’ll run, noise at that speed, what it costs to keep running, and who has tested it independently. Then do the math for your room.
