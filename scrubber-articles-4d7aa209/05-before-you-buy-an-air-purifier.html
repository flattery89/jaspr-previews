Air purifiers: what to know before you buy

A plain guide to buying an air purifier: airflow, noise, running cost, independent testing, returns and warranty, plus a checklist for buying online.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]7 min read

The loudest number on a listing often isn’t the one that fits your room.

You open a listing. The first thing you see is a top-speed airflow figure. Next to it sits a square-foot rating, then a filter percentage with no test method beside it. Each can be true and still tell you very little about the machine that will run in your room.

This guide gives you four checks that don’t depend on any one brand: airflow and noise at the speed you will actually run, running cost, independent testing, and return and warranty terms. It ends with a checklist for buying online and the red flags to look for. Do those, and a listing stops being a puzzle.

Every example number comes from a maker’s own product page, read in October 2026. Prices move, so treat them as illustrations of what to look for, not a shopping list.

1. Match airflow and noise to the speed you will run

Airflow: read the CADR, and ask which speed

CADR, the clean air delivery rate, says how much air a unit cleans of a given particle. On the US standard it is measured in cubic feet per minute (CFM) and reported separately for smoke, dust and pollen, from smaller particles to larger. A bigger number means more air moved.

Then ask the question most pages skip: at which speed? Many publish one figure and no speed. One maker lists a smoke CADR of 340 CFM with no speed stated. Another lists 253. IQAir publishes six speeds, from 40 to 300 CFM, with the noise at each. That is the format to look for, because you won’t run a unit flat out.

Air changes per hour: the number you can calculate

Air changes per hour counts how many times a room’s air passes through the filter. The formula is airflow in CFM times 60, divided by the room’s volume in cubic feet.

A 250 square foot room with an 8-foot ceiling holds 2,000 cubic feet. A unit moving 100 CFM at the speed you will run gives 3.0 changes an hour. One moving 250 CFM gives 7.5. Many guides suggest four to five an hour, roughly where the industry’s room-size convention lands at 8 feet. It is a yardstick, not a guarantee. Furniture, doors and the unit’s position change how a room mixes.

Room-size ratings can’t be compared

Square-foot ratings rest on an assumed air-change rate, and makers pick different ones. Coway lists its Airmega 250 at 1,860 square feet at one change an hour, 930 at two and 465 at four: one machine, three room sizes. AirDoctor’s 3500 says 630 square feet at four times an hour. Shark’s HP302 says "up to 1,400 sq. ft. in just one hour" and gives no airflow figure. Convert each to CFM before you compare.

Noise: the speed, the distance and the type

A noise figure needs three things beside it: the speed, the distance and whether it is sound pressure or sound power. Pressure is what a meter in your room reads, and it changes with distance. Power is what the machine gives off. They don’t line up.

Winix lists 23.5 dB on its slowest speed. IQAir lists 27 to 59 dB(A) at 3.3 feet across six speeds. Dyson’s spec sheet lists sound power. Each is real, and none compares directly with the others. Ask for noise at the same speed as the airflow figure. As a rough guide, each 10 dB reads to most ears as about twice as loud.

2. Add up the running cost

For many purifiers the unit is the smaller number. Filters are the bill that keeps coming. The formula: filter price, divided by months of life, times 12.

Examples from the makers’ own pages: Winix lists a $49.99 filter for its 5510. Coway lists $89 for the Airmega 250’s, with a one-year life in its spec table. AirDoctor lists $171.99 for a one-year combo pack for its 3500. Jaspr lists $199 every six months on its filter subscription, or $398 a year. That is roughly eight to one from cheapest to dearest.

Check how life is stated. Some makers give months, others hours. Dyson lists its HEPA filter at 21,900 hours, about 2.5 years if the unit never switches off, and its carbon filter at 8,760 hours, about one year. Hours help once you know how many you will run.

Electricity is smaller but real: watts times hours, divided by 1,000, times your rate. Coway lists 64 W for the Airmega 250, about 46 kilowatt-hours a month if it never stops. Ask whether the warranty depends on a subscription and whether you can pause it. Then take the unit price and add five years of filters. That is the number to compare, not the sticker.

3. Look for independent testing

Not all proof is equal. A useful way to sort it:

  • Industry verification. Some makers show an AHAM Verified mark, such as Winix on its 392 square foot rating.
  • Public lists. ENERGY STAR keeps a list of certified models. It held 247 when we downloaded it on October 5, 2026. Plenty of good units aren’t on it, so absence isn’t a verdict. Presence is easy to check.
  • Lab reports. Anyone can print a percentage. A trustworthy report names the lab, the date, what was released, the chamber size, the temperature and humidity, the sampling interval, a run with the unit switched off to compare against, and the fan speed.

One example is Jaspr’s mold report. LMS Technologies, a microbiology lab in Bloomington, Minnesota, released Aspergillus niger into a 4,000 cubic foot chamber at 71°F and 45% humidity, sampled every five minutes and compared the unit against a run with it off. Corrected removal reached 97.1% at 60 minutes and 99.8% at 120. It doesn’t state the fan speed. The maker publishes no filtration grade, so this report is the evidence on offer.

A lab chamber isn’t your living room; results depend on the room, the mold and how it’s measured. Air purifiers are not a substitute for mold remediation. And a claim like "99.97% at 0.3 microns" describes a filter material under a test method, not your room.

4. Read the return and warranty terms

Returns

Thirty days is a common window. The details matter more. Who pays shipping on a return, when the unit weighs 20 to 25 pounds? Is there a restocking fee? Coway’s refund page says you cover shipping and handling on refunds unless the return is Coway’s error, and deducts at least 15% of the price if parts are missing. Do you need the original box, proof of purchase or a video of the problem?

Warranty

Look at the length, what is covered and the conditions. Coway lists one year on internal parts and three or five on motor and electronics, depending on the model. AirDoctor lists one year, and five with an active filter subscription. IQAir lists one year, and ten if the unit is registered within 90 days and the owner enrolls in a filter subscription or shows genuine filter purchases. Jaspr’s lifetime warranty works the same way: it lasts while you stay on its filter subscription and change the filter every six months.

When you see "lifetime," ask whose lifetime and on what condition. Check whether third-party filters void the cover. Coway’s warranty excludes non-genuine filters.

5. A checklist for buying online

Online listings are where the numbers get thin and the lookalikes appear. Work down this list before you pay.

Check the seller and the model

  • Buy from the maker’s own store or a named, authorized seller. On a marketplace, check who ships and who sells.
  • Match the listing’s model number to the maker’s spec sheet. Near-identical names often hide different units.
  • Look up the replacement filter and its price before you buy the unit.

Check the numbers

  • Find the manual or spec sheet on the maker’s site and compare it to the listing.
  • Note the units. Airflow is sometimes shown in cubic meters an hour; one is about 0.59 CFM.
  • Write down three figures per unit: CFM at the speed you will run, dB at that speed and filter cost a year.

Red flags on a listing

  • Airflow at top speed, with no speed named.
  • A square-foot rating with no air-change rate.
  • A filter claim with no test method, or phrases like "HEPA-type" and "medical-grade."
  • Noise given for one speed alone, usually the slowest.
  • No filter price on the product page.
  • A "lifetime" warranty with the conditions out of sight.
  • Promises about how you will feel or what the unit will treat.
  • A feature that makes ozone, such as an ionizer, with no emissions certification.
  • Reviews that are all recent, or none that mention a filter change.

Before you pay, screenshot the specs and the return and warranty terms, put the end of the return window in your calendar and keep the box until it closes.

The short version

The right purifier is the one whose weak spot you can live with. Pick the speed you will run, find the airflow and noise at that speed, add up five years of filters and read the return terms before you pay. If a listing hides any of those four, that tells you something too.
