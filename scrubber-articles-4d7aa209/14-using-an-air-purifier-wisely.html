How to use an air purifier wisely: placement, run time and room size

Where an air cleaner sits, how long it runs and how well it fits the room decide how much it does. A practical guide with the math for your own room.

By the Jaspr team[Publish date]6 min read

A modest air cleaner in the right spot can outwork a strong one in the wrong.

You carry the box in, peel off the plastic and look for the nearest outlet. That is often behind the sofa, or at the end of a hallway, or in whatever corner the cord happens to reach. The unit hums along there for months, and the room never tells you whether it’s doing much.

Three small decisions settle that: how to size a unit to the room, where to put it and how long to run it. None of this needs a degree. It needs a tape measure, ten minutes with a calculator and a few habits, and the advice applies to any room unit, whatever the box calls it.

One idea runs underneath all of it. A filter cleans the air that moves through it, and nothing else. Every tip below is a way of getting more of your room’s air through the filter, or of keeping less being added.

Size it to the room, not the square footage

Measure length, width and ceiling height, and multiply. A 12 by 14 foot bedroom with an 8-foot ceiling is 1,344 cubic feet. That is the volume of air the unit has to work through.

Then find the unit’s airflow in CFM at the speed you would actually run, and work out air changes per hour: CFM × 60 ÷ room volume. A unit moving 114 CFM gives about 5.1 changes an hour in that bedroom (114 × 60 ÷ 1,344). In a 250 square foot living room with an 8-foot ceiling, the same unit gives about 3.4.

Square-foot ratings skip all of that, so treat them as a rough first filter and then do the math. If you are choosing between two sizes, size to the room where you spend the most hours.

Ceiling height matters more than people expect. Raise the ceiling in that 250 square foot living room from 8 feet to 10 and the same 114 CFM falls from about 3.4 changes an hour to about 2.7, because the room holds a quarter more air. A vaulted room does worse again.

Open-plan spaces change the sum. A 250 square foot room that opens into a 250 square foot kitchen is 4,000 cubic feet, not 2,000, and the same 114 CFM now gives about 1.7 changes an hour instead of 3.4. In a space like that, either choose a unit with more airflow or accept that it works hardest near where it sits.

Where to put it

  • Give the intake some room. Most units want clearance on the sides that take air in, so check the manual for the number. A unit pushed against a wall or tucked behind a sofa is starved of air. One that draws from all around its base, as Jaspr’s does, still works better with open floor around it .
  • Put it where the air is. In a bedroom, a few feet from the bed rather than in the far corner behind the door. In a kitchen or open-plan living area, between the source and where you sit, not right beside the stove.
  • Mind the doors. A closed door turns a bedroom into its own small volume, which makes the unit’s job easier. An open door turns it into the bedroom plus the hallway.
  • Keep sensors away from steam. Units with air-quality sensors read what is next to them. Owners of sensor-equipped units say the reading can turn orange or red near a humidifier, so give the unit some distance from one .
  • Avoid dead corners. A unit jammed into a corner has air coming from fewer directions. A spot a few feet along a wall gives it more to work with.

How long to run it

Run it continuously, at a speed you can ignore. A unit that is switched off while you cook, clean or have guests over misses the moments the air is changing most, and particles don’t stop arriving when you stop looking. That is also your nose’s trap: it stops registering a smell within minutes, while the source carries on.

That second point is why speed matters less than you would think. Pick the speed you will leave on. A quiet setting that runs all day does more than a loud one you turn off after an hour. Look up the noise at that speed in dBA, since the figure on the box is often the lowest speed. If you can hear it, you will switch it off.

Speed changes the numbers a lot. In that 12 by 14 foot bedroom, a unit at 114 CFM gives about 5.1 changes an hour and the same unit at 340 CFM gives about 15.2. The higher speed is louder, so most people keep it for bursts rather than every hour. Leave the lower speed on as the baseline.

Auto modes help. A built-in sensor can raise the fan when cooking or hairspray adds particles and lower it when the air settles. They are as good as the sensor, and the screen is not proof (more on that below).

Turn it up on purpose when you know you are adding something: cooking, a dusty project, a full house. Then let it settle back down.

Running all day does use electricity, so check the unit’s wattage at the speed you will use and multiply by the hours and your local rate. A low speed on a modest motor is a small line on the bill, and that is the speed that should carry most of the hours.

Room by room

  • Bedroom. A few feet from the bed, door closed, on the lowest speed whose noise you can ignore. If the unit has a display, use its dark mode so there is no glow after the lights go out.
  • Kitchen or open-plan living area. Between the stove and the seating, not right beside the stove. Expect an auto mode to raise the fan during cooking, and let it.
  • Home office. Near where you sit, with the door closed if you can. The hours are long, so noise at the speed you run matters more than a top-speed rating.
  • Basement or utility room. Keep the unit off any floor that gets wet. If the room is damp, fix the moisture first, because the air will not stay clean while the source is still adding to it.

Keep the filter fresh

A loaded filter lets less air through, and a unit that moves less air changes the room’s air less often. Replace filters on the maker’s schedule, and sooner after a renovation or in a house full of pets. Set a reminder the day you buy it. A unit that is cheap to buy and expensive to feed gets neglected.

Three mistakes worth avoiding

  • Trusting the screen. A display reads one sensor in one spot, and it can say "Excellent" in a room with a problem it doesn’t measure. For proof, use a separate instrument such as a handheld particle counter, and measure before and after.
  • Expecting it to fix the source. A leak, a damp wall or a stove with a weak vent hood keeps adding. If the problem is damp or mold, fix the moisture and have the mold remediated. Air purifiers are not a substitute for mold remediation; an air cleaner works on the air while that happens.
  • Moving it every few days. Placement matters less than consistency. Pick a sensible spot, leave it there and let the unit run.
  • Sizing by the sticker. A rating that says "up to 400 square feet" tells you nothing about your ceiling, your speed or your noise limit. Do the math.

A five-line checklist

  1. Work out the room’s volume and the air changes at the speed you will run.
  2. Leave open floor around the unit and keep sensors away from steam.
  3. Put it where you spend your hours, with the door closed if you can.
  4. Leave it running on a speed you can’t hear.
  5. Change the filter on schedule.
